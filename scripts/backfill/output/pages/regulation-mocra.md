## What Is MoCRA?

The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 (MoCRA) is the most significant expansion of FDA authority over cosmetics since the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. Signed into law on December 29, 2022, as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 (Public Law 117-328), MoCRA fundamentally restructures the regulatory framework for cosmetics sold in the United States.

Before MoCRA, the FDA had almost no mandatory authority over cosmetics. No facility registration. No product listing. No adverse event reporting requirement. No mandatory recall power. The cosmetics industry was, by a wide margin, the least regulated consumer product category under the FDA's jurisdiction. A company could manufacture and sell a cosmetic product without ever notifying the FDA that it existed.

MoCRA changes that. It establishes mandatory facility registration, product listing, serious adverse event reporting, safety substantiation, Good Manufacturing Practice requirements, and gives the FDA mandatory recall authority for the first time. It also requires new labeling provisions, fragrance allergen disclosure, and a congressional report on PFAS in cosmetics.

**Why it matters for the industry:** MoCRA affects every company that manufactures, processes, packs, or distributes cosmetic products in the United States. That includes domestic manufacturers, contract manufacturers, foreign exporters, brand owners, and private label companies. The law applies regardless of company size, although small businesses (under $1 million in average annual cosmetic sales) receive limited exemptions from certain provisions.


## Current Regulatory Status

MoCRA was enacted on December 29, 2022. Several provisions took effect immediately or on statutory deadlines. Others require FDA rulemaking that has been significantly delayed.

**Provisions currently in effect:**

- **Serious adverse event reporting** (effective December 29, 2023): Responsible persons must report serious adverse events to the FDA within 15 business days and submit any follow-up medical information received within one year.
- **Facility registration and product listing** (enforced from July 1, 2024): All cosmetic manufacturing and processing facilities must register with the FDA. All marketed products must be listed with their full ingredient declarations. As of January 1, 2025, the FDA reported 9,528 active facility registrations and 589,762 unique product listings.
- **Responsible person labeling** (effective December 29, 2024): Product packaging must include the name and contact information of the responsible person (the manufacturer, packer, or distributor whose name appears on the label).
- **Safety substantiation** (effective December 29, 2023): Responsible persons must ensure and maintain records supporting that there is adequate evidence of safety for each marketed cosmetic product.
- **Mandatory recall authority** (effective December 29, 2022): The FDA has the authority to order a mandatory recall of any cosmetic that is adulterated or misbranded and poses a risk of serious adverse health consequences or death, if the responsible person refuses to recall voluntarily.
- **Records access** (effective December 29, 2022): The FDA can access records related to a cosmetic product if there is a reasonable belief that it is adulterated and presents a threat of serious adverse health consequences.

**Provisions pending rulemaking:**

- **Good Manufacturing Practices**: The statutory deadline for a proposed rule was December 29, 2024, and a final rule by December 29, 2025. Both deadlines have passed without action. As of the Fall 2025 Unified Agenda, GMP rulemaking is classified as a "long-term action" with no target date for a proposed rule. A final GMP rule is not expected before late 2026 at the earliest.
- **Fragrance allergen labeling**: The statutory deadline for this proposed rule was June 29, 2024 (18 months after enactment). The FDA missed that deadline, set a revised target of January 2025 in the Spring 2024 Unified Agenda, and has now delayed further to May 2026.
- **Standardized asbestos testing for talc**: The FDA issued a proposed rule in December 2024 but withdrew it in November 2025 citing the need to "reconsider the best means of addressing the issues." The agency stated it would reissue a new proposed rule to meet its statutory obligations, but no timeline has been set.

**Political and regulatory headwinds:** Executive Order 14192, issued by the Trump Administration, imposes a "one-in, ten-out" requirement for new regulations, which has further delayed MoCRA rulemaking. However, the core statutory requirements (registration, listing, adverse event reporting, recall authority) are self-executing provisions that do not require additional rulemaking to be enforceable.

## Detailed Analysis

### Facility Registration and Product Listing

MoCRA requires every facility that manufactures or processes cosmetic products for distribution in the United States to register with the FDA. "Facility" includes both domestic and foreign establishments. Registration must include the facility name, address, contact information, the types of cosmetics manufactured, and the brand names under which products are marketed.

Product listing requires submitting the product name, category, ingredient list (including fragrance and flavor components by their common names), and the responsible person's contact information. Listings must be submitted electronically through the FDA's Structured Product Labeling (SPL) system.

As of January 2025, the FDA has received 9,528 active facility registrations and 589,762 product listings. These numbers provide the agency with the first comprehensive view of the U.S. cosmetics market it has ever had.

**Key compliance details:**
- Existing facilities were required to register by July 1, 2024 (extended from the original December 29, 2023 deadline)
- New facilities must register within 60 days of beginning operations
- Registrations must be renewed biennially
- Small businesses with less than $1 million in average annual cosmetic sales are exempt from registration and listing requirements
- The exemption does not apply to products that contact mucous membranes of the eye, are injected, are intended for internal use, or alter appearance for more than 24 hours

On December 11, 2024, the FDA released final guidance on facility registration and product listing, including updated FAQs clarifying submission requirements, facility definitions, and ingredient disclosure obligations.

### Serious Adverse Event Reporting

MoCRA's adverse event reporting provision is one of its most immediately impactful requirements. Before MoCRA, cosmetics companies had no obligation to report adverse events to the FDA. Now, responsible persons must:

1. **Report within 15 business days** of receiving information about a serious adverse event associated with use of a cosmetic product in the United States
2. **Submit follow-up information** received within one year of the initial report, within 15 business days of receiving it
3. **Include a copy of the product label** from the retail packaging with each report
4. **Maintain records** for six years (three years for small businesses)

A "serious adverse event" is defined as one that results in death, a life-threatening experience, inpatient hospitalization, a persistent or significant disability or incapacity, a congenital anomaly or birth defect, an infection, or significant disfigurement requiring medical or surgical intervention.

The FDA issued updated reporting instructions in 2024, specifying the use of MedWatch Form FDA 3500A for mandatory cosmetics adverse event reporting. Reports must be submitted to the FDA's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN).

### Good Manufacturing Practices

MoCRA directed the FDA to propose cosmetic GMP regulations by December 29, 2024, and finalize them by December 29, 2025. Both deadlines have passed without action.

The Fall 2025 Unified Agenda moved GMP rulemaking to "long-term actions" with no proposed rule date, signaling the FDA does not expect to issue a proposed rule within the next year. At best, a final GMP rule is not expected before late 2026.

In the interim, the FDA has encouraged companies to align with ISO 22716:2007 (Cosmetics — Good Manufacturing Practices), which is the internationally recognized standard for cosmetic GMP. The FDA's 2013 draft guidance on cosmetic GMP also remains available as a reference, though it predates MoCRA and is not binding.

**What companies should do now:** Map current quality systems against likely requirements of a future 21 CFR 711 framework. Key areas to address include personnel qualifications, facility design and maintenance, equipment cleaning and calibration, raw material testing, in-process controls, finished product testing, documentation and recordkeeping, and complaint handling. Companies already operating under ISO 22716 are likely well-positioned for eventual FDA GMP requirements.

### Fragrance Allergen Disclosure

MoCRA requires the FDA to issue regulations mandating the disclosure of individual fragrance allergens on cosmetic labels. This provision addresses a significant transparency gap — under current law, companies can list "fragrance" as a single ingredient without disclosing the specific chemicals that comprise it, even when those chemicals are known contact allergens.

The proposed rule was originally targeted for January 2025 but has been delayed to May 2026. The EU already requires disclosure of 26 (soon expanding to 80+) individual fragrance allergens on cosmetic labels under Regulation (EC) No. 1223/2009, and MoCRA's provision is expected to bring U.S. requirements closer to the EU approach.

### PFAS in Cosmetics

MoCRA required the FDA to submit a report to Congress on the use of PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) in cosmetics and associated health risks. The FDA published this report on December 29, 2025, meeting its statutory deadline.

**Key findings from the PFAS report:**

- Based on mandatory product listing data, **51 PFAS are used in 1,744 cosmetic formulations** marketed in the United States
- The FDA evaluated the 25 most frequently used PFAS, representing approximately 96% of PFAS intentionally added to cosmetics
- **Five PFAS appeared to present low safety concerns** under their intended conditions of use
- **The safety of most PFAS could not be definitively established** due to data gaps
- **One PFAS was identified as having a potential safety concern** with significant remaining uncertainty
- The report identified critical data gaps: limited information on use levels, insufficient data on dermal and oral absorption, lack of mechanistic understanding, and absence of dermal toxicity data

The FDA's conclusion was notably cautious: there are currently no federal regulations specifically prohibiting PFAS intentionally added to cosmetic products, and the report did not recommend new restrictions. However, several states have already enacted or proposed PFAS bans in cosmetics, creating a patchwork of state-level requirements.

### Mandatory Recall Authority

For the first time, MoCRA gives the FDA the power to mandate a recall of cosmetic products. Previously, all cosmetic recalls were voluntary. Under Section 611 of the FD&C Act (as amended by MoCRA), the FDA can order a mandatory recall when:

1. There is a reasonable probability that the cosmetic is adulterated or misbranded, AND
2. There is a reasonable probability that use of or exposure to the cosmetic will cause serious adverse health consequences or death

Before issuing a mandatory recall order, the FDA must provide the responsible person written notice and an opportunity to voluntarily cease distribution. After issuing an order, the company has the right to an informal hearing within 10 days.

On December 18, 2025, the FDA published draft guidance clarifying its mandatory recall procedures. The FDA has stated that most violative cosmetics will continue to be recalled voluntarily, but companies that refuse may face mandatory recall orders, injunctions, or criminal prosecution.

### Safety Substantiation

MoCRA requires responsible persons to ensure and maintain records demonstrating adequate evidence of safety for each cosmetic product. This is a significant shift from the pre-MoCRA era, when no federal law explicitly required companies to substantiate the safety of cosmetic products before marketing them.

The law does not define a specific testing protocol but requires that safety evidence be adequate. Industry generally interprets this as requiring a combination of ingredient-level toxicology assessments, formulation stability testing, and, where appropriate, clinical or consumer use testing.


## Affected Product Categories

MoCRA applies broadly to all products meeting the FD&C Act's definition of "cosmetic" — articles intended to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled, or sprayed on, introduced into, or otherwise applied to the human body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance.

**Primary categories affected:**
- **Skin care**: Moisturizers, serums, cleansers, toners, masks, anti-aging products
- **Personal cleanliness**: Soaps (beyond the "true soap" exemption), body washes, hand sanitizers with cosmetic claims
- **Baby products**: Baby lotions, shampoos, powders, diaper creams
- **Suntan preparations**: Self-tanners, bronzers (not sunscreens, which are regulated as OTC drugs)
- **Hair care**: Shampoos, conditioners, styling products, hair dyes, relaxers
- **Makeup and color cosmetics**: Foundation, lipstick, mascara, eye shadow, blush
- **Nail products**: Polish, gel systems, acrylic systems, cuticle treatments
- **Fragrances**: Perfumes, colognes, body sprays

**OTC drug/cosmetic hybrids**: Products that are both cosmetics and OTC drugs (e.g., anti-dandruff shampoos, fluoride toothpastes, antiperspirants, sunscreens, acne treatments) must comply with both MoCRA and existing OTC drug requirements. Several warning letters issued in 2025 targeted manufacturers of cosmetic/drug combination products for GMP violations.

**Small business exemption**: Companies with less than $1 million in average annual cosmetic sales (averaged over the previous three years, adjusted for inflation) are exempt from facility registration, product listing, and GMP requirements. However, they are NOT exempt from adverse event reporting, safety substantiation, labeling requirements, or the FDA's recall authority. The exemption also does not apply to products contacting eye mucous membranes, injectables, internal-use products, or products altering appearance for more than 24 hours.


## State-Level Cosmetics Regulations

While MoCRA establishes a new federal baseline, several states have enacted cosmetics regulations that go beyond MoCRA's requirements. MoCRA does not preempt state laws, meaning companies must comply with both federal and any applicable state requirements.

### California

California is the most aggressive state regulator of cosmetics:

- **Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act (AB 2762)**: Effective January 1, 2025, bans 24 ingredients from cosmetic products, including all PFAS as a class, mercury, formaldehyde, and several other substances already banned by the EU.
- **Assembly Bill 496**: Signed October 2023, adds 26 more banned substances effective January 1, 2027.
- **Cosmetic Fragrance and Flavor Ingredient Right to Know Act (SB 312)**: Since January 2022, requires companies to report fragrance and flavor ingredients linked to health or environmental harm to the California Safe Cosmetics Program Database.
- **Proposition 65**: Requires warnings for products containing chemicals known to cause cancer or reproductive harm, including many substances used in cosmetics.

### Other States

Several other states have enacted or proposed cosmetics ingredient restrictions, particularly targeting PFAS. This creates an increasingly complex patchwork of state-level requirements that companies must navigate alongside MoCRA's federal framework.

### EU Comparison

The European Union's Cosmetics Regulation (EC No. 1223/2009) remains significantly more restrictive than MoCRA. The EU bans or restricts over 2,400 substances in cosmetics, compared to the handful restricted at the U.S. federal level. MoCRA closes some of the gap — particularly around adverse event reporting, facility registration, and GMP — but does not approach the EU's substance-specific restrictions or its precautionary regulatory philosophy.


## FDA Enforcement Actions Under MoCRA

The FDA has begun enforcing MoCRA provisions, issuing warning letters at a pace consistent with prior administrations. During the first two months of the Trump Administration (January–March 2025), the FDA issued warning letters to three entities for cosmetics-related violations.

Notable enforcement actions relevant to MoCRA compliance include:

- **Warning letters to cosmetic/drug manufacturers** for GMP violations at manufacturing facilities, including failures in quality control oversight and distribution of adulterated products containing elevated impurities
- **Warning letters for labeling violations**, including failure to include the responsible person's contact information as required by MoCRA
- **Draft guidance on mandatory recalls** (December 18, 2025), signaling the FDA's readiness to use its new recall authority when voluntary action is insufficient

The FDA has signaled that enforcement will intensify as more provisions take effect and as the agency builds its compliance infrastructure using the facility registration and product listing data it is now collecting for the first time.


## What Companies Should Do

### Immediate Actions (Already Required)

1. **Verify facility registration**: Confirm your facility is registered with the FDA and that your registration information is current. First biennial renewals are due in July 2026.
2. **Confirm product listings**: Ensure all marketed products are listed with complete ingredient declarations via the SPL system.
3. **Establish adverse event reporting procedures**: Implement a system to identify, evaluate, and report serious adverse events within 15 business days. Train customer service and quality teams on what constitutes a "serious adverse event."
4. **Update labeling**: Verify that the responsible person's name and contact information appears on all product packaging.
5. **Document safety substantiation**: Maintain records supporting the safety of each marketed product. This should include ingredient-level safety assessments and finished product evaluations.

### Near-Term Preparation (2026)

6. **Prepare for GMP compliance**: Even though the final rule is delayed, begin mapping your quality systems against ISO 22716 and anticipated FDA requirements. Companies that wait for the final rule will face a compressed compliance timeline.
7. **Monitor fragrance allergen rulemaking**: If your products contain fragrance, prepare for potential disclosure requirements. Ensure your supply chain can provide component-level ingredient data for fragrance compositions.
8. **Assess PFAS exposure**: Review your product formulations for intentionally added PFAS. Cross-reference against the 51 PFAS identified in the FDA's December 2025 report. Consider reformulation strategies, particularly for products sold in states with PFAS bans.

### Strategic Considerations

9. **Track state regulations**: MoCRA does not preempt state laws. Companies selling in California must comply with the Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act, AB 496, SB 312, and Proposition 65 in addition to MoCRA. Build a regulatory monitoring system that covers both federal and state requirements.
10. **Audit contract manufacturers**: MoCRA responsibilities fall on the "responsible person," which is typically the brand owner, not the contract manufacturer. Ensure your manufacturing partners can support your compliance obligations, particularly around adverse event reporting, GMP documentation, and ingredient transparency.
11. **Prepare for increased FDA scrutiny**: The FDA now has visibility into the cosmetics market through facility registrations and product listings that it never had before. Companies should expect more inspections, more information requests, and more enforcement actions as the agency builds institutional capacity in cosmetics regulation.

MoCRA is not a future concern. Its core provisions are law. The companies that treat it as an active compliance obligation today will be better positioned than those waiting for the GMP rule to finalize before taking action.
